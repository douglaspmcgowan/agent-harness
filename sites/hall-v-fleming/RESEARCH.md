# Hall v. Fleming — research notes

Primary source: *Bethany M. Hall v. A. Scott Fleming*, No. 25-1574 (4th Cir. May 13, 2026)
(published). Full opinion text extracted from the CourtListener PDF and read in full.

Everything in this section is drawn directly from the opinion. Claims sourced from news
coverage are kept separate, in `COVERAGE.md`, and marked with their outlet.

## Caption and posture

- **Parties:** Bethany M. Hall, Plaintiff–Appellant, v. A. Scott Fleming, Defendant–Appellee.
- **Fleming's role:** sued in his official capacity as director of the State Council of Higher
  Education for Virginia ("SCHEV"), which administers the grant program.
- **Claim:** a single count under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 alleging violation of her Free Exercise
  rights under the First Amendment.
- **Below:** E.D. Va. (Richmond), District Judge David J. Novak, No. 3:25-cv-00186-DJN,
  reported at 2025 WL 2021022 (E.D. Va. May 9, 2025). Motion to dismiss granted **with prejudice**.
- **Appellate jurisdiction:** 28 U.S.C. § 1291.
- **Argued:** December 10, 2025. **Decided:** May 13, 2026.
- **Panel:** Agee, Richardson, and Benjamin, Circuit Judges.
- **Disposition:** Affirmed by published opinion. Judge Benjamin wrote the opinion; Agee and
  Richardson joined. Judge Richardson also wrote a concurrence. (Unanimous as to judgment.)

## The program at issue

- **Virginia Tuition Assistance Grant Program ("VTAG").** Financial assistance to Virginia
  residents attending accredited, private, nonprofit colleges and universities in Virginia.
- **Amount:** up to **$5,000 per school year**.
- **Eligibility:** students must be "obligated to pay tuition as full-time . . . students at an
  eligible institution." Va. Code Ann. § 23.1-631 (2016).
- **The exclusion:** eligibility is limited to "nonprofit private institutions of higher
  education whose primary purpose is to provide collegiate, graduate, or professional education
  and **not provide religious training or theological education**." Va. Code Ann. § 23.1-628 (2016).
- **The regulation:** undergraduate "[p]rograms that provide religious training or theological
  education, classified as **CIP Code 39-series** programs, are not eligible programs."
  8 Va. Admin. Code § 40-71-10 (2022).
- **CIP** = Classification of Instructional Programs (National Center for Education Statistics).
  CIP Code 39 lists majors that "prepare individuals for the professional practice of religious
  vocations."
- **Important nuance the court stressed:** students at religious institutions *are* eligible —
  Liberty, Regent, and Eastern Mennonite students can all participate. The exclusion is by
  **major**, not by school. And a **double major** combining an eligible major with a CIP 39
  major stays eligible, so long as in any given semester the student takes no more credits in
  the ineligible major than in the eligible one. The court called VTAG's criteria "actually
  broader than the Washington statute in Locke" (n.6).

## The legal question

Hall **conceded** her situation matched *Locke v. Davey*, 540 U.S. 712 (2004) — the district
court noted she agreed "her situation aligns with the one faced by the undergraduate student
pursuing a religious vocation major in Locke," and on appeal her reply brief conceded the facts
"are on all fours [or identical]" with *Locke*.

So the sole question was whether *Trinity Lutheran* (2017), *Espinoza* (2020), and *Carson*
(2022) had abrogated *Locke* — in her phrase, "into oblivion." The panel: **"They did not."**

### The majority's reasoning (Benjamin, J.)

- *Locke*: Washington's scholarship excluded students pursuing a degree in theology. Joshua
  Davey double-majored in pastoral ministries and business. The Supreme Court held no Free
  Exercise violation — the state "merely chose[] not to fund a distinct category of instruction,"
  and the program was backed by a "historic and substantial state interest" "against procuring
  taxpayer funds to support church leaders."
- *Trinity Lutheran* (playground resurfacing): struck down a policy disqualifying an applicant
  "solely because of their religious **character**." But it expressly distinguished *Locke* —
  Washington had "merely chosen not to fund a distinct category of instruction," and its choice
  was "in keeping with the State's antiestablishment interest in not using taxpayer funds to
  pay for the training of clergy." So it **reaffirmed** *Locke*.
- *Espinoza*: Montana barred aid to religious schools "simply because of what it is." That
  "turn[ed] expressly on religious **status** and not religious **use**." Again distinguished
  *Locke*, again invoking the "historic and substantial" interest.
- *Carson*: Maine's "nonsectarian" requirement fell. But the Court again said *Locke* "could 'be
  of no help to Maine'" — in *Locke* "only a 'vocational religious' degree was excluded." And
  **"for the third time"** the Court emphasized the historic interest against using "taxpayer
  funds to support church leaders."
- Hall's *Lemon* analogy rejected: with *Lemon*, the Supreme Court made explicit statements
  abandoning it (*American Legion*, *Kennedy* — "long ago abandoned Lemon"). Nothing comparable
  has been said about *Locke*.
- Controlling rule: *Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/American Express*, 490 U.S. 477, 484 (1989)
  — a lower court must "follow the case which directly controls, leaving to [the Supreme] Court
  the prerogative of overruling its own decisions."

**Holding:** "we disagree with Hall and hold that the Supreme Court has yet to either overrule
or abandon its decision in Locke through Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza, or Carson." Affirmed.

### The concurrence (Richardson, J.) — the striking part

Richardson joined the majority in full and then wrote to say *Locke* is wrong:

- "I concur because *Locke v. Davey* . . . controls here. I write separately because *Locke* was
  wrongly decided. It betrays the founding generation's commitment to religious liberty, and the
  Supreme Court should formally overrule it. But, until the Court does, *Locke* binds us — **even
  as a moth-eaten shell of its former self.**"
- On the history: adopts Scalia's *Locke* dissent — the founding-era record involved "laws that
  singled [ministers] out for financial aid," not their inclusion in general benefit programs.
  Quotes Scalia: "No one would seriously contend . . . that the Framers would have barred
  ministers from using public roads on their way to church."
- Footnote 1, on Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority: **"Even a great Chief Justice can err."**
- On precedent: *Locke* should have been controlled by *Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City
  of Hialeah*, and the *Locke* Court "resolved the case without applying any particular level of
  scrutiny."
- On erosion: *Carson* held status- and use-based discrimination equally "offensive to the Free
  Exercise Clause" and "cabined *Locke* to its facts: '*Locke* cannot be read beyond its narrow
  focus on vocational religious degrees.'" "At this point, the Supreme Court has rejected or
  ignored every premise of *Locke* except its flawed historical analysis."
- The counterfactual: "**Absent *Locke*, the Court's more recent decisions in *Trinity Lutheran*,
  *Espinoza*, and *Carson* would require us to hold that Virginia's program violates the Free
  Exercise Clause.** But we are not yet free to ignore *Locke*."
- On vertical stare decisis: quotes Amy Coney Barrett, *Precedent and Jurisprudential
  Disagreement*, 91 Tex. L. Rev. 1711, 1712 (2013) — "Vertical stare decisis is an inflexible
  rule that admits of no exception."
- **The murderer/coroner footnote (n.5):** overruling a case is like murder; "the Supreme Court
  is the only acceptable murderer in our system of strict vertical stare decisis. Yet the
  murderer is different from the coroner. The coroner declares the case dead. The question . . .
  is not whether lower courts can be murderers — they obviously can't. Instead, the question is
  whether the Supreme Court is the sole coroner, or whether lower courts may sometimes assume
  that role."
- Closing: "***Locke v. Davey* is a stain on our Free Exercise jurisprudence.** And the Supreme
  Court has all but confined it to its facts. But until the Court formally buries *Locke*, we
  lower-court judges must keep applying it."

## Why the framing diverges (the hook for the coverage comparison)

This decision is unusually easy to report in opposite directions **without anyone lying**:

- **Outcome frame:** the student lost; Virginia's exclusion stands; *Locke* is still good law.
- **Reasoning frame:** all three judges signalled *Locke* is barely alive, and one wrote that
  the program would be *unconstitutional* but for a precedent he called a "stain" and a
  "moth-eaten shell."

Both are accurate readings of the same 18 pages. A headline can therefore be "court upholds ban
on taxpayer money for pastoral degrees" or "judge says binding precedent is a stain that should
be overruled," and neither is fabricated. That divergence is the subject of the site.
