The case & the coverage Interview prep

Fourth Circuit · decided 13 May 2026

One ruling,
two true headlines.

A Liberty University student lost her challenge to Virginia's rule that tuition grants don't follow you into a ministry major. Every judge on the panel agreed she lost. One of them also wrote that the precedent forcing that result is “a stain” that the Supreme Court should overrule.

Both of those are the same eighteen pages. This page lays out what the court actually said, how the case got here, and how differently outlets told it.

Bethany M. Hall v. A. Scott Fleming
No. 25-1574 · U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit
On appeal from E.D. Va. (Richmond), No. 3:25-cv-00186-DJN
Argued 10 Dec 2025 · Decided 13 May 2026 · Published
Before AGEE, RICHARDSON, and BENJAMIN, Circuit Judges

The split screen

Two headlines, neither of them wrong

This decision is unusually easy to report in opposite directions without anyone fabricating a thing. The outcome and the reasoning point different ways.

Outcome frame

Court upholds Virginia's bar on tuition aid for ministry majors

Accurate. The panel affirmed dismissal, unanimously. Virginia's exclusion stands, Locke v. Davey remains binding law, and Hall gets nothing. The case was dismissed with prejudice below and that dismissal was affirmed.

Reasoning frame

Judge: the precedent I'm applying is “a stain” and should be overruled

Also accurate. Judge Richardson concurred to say that absent Locke, recent Supreme Court cases “would require us to hold that Virginia's program violates the Free Exercise Clause,” and called Locke “a moth-eaten shell of its former self.”

An outlet choosing one frame over the other isn't necessarily spinning. But a reader who sees only one comes away with a materially different sense of where this area of law is heading.

Visual history · the doctrine

How much of Locke was left by the time Hall got there

Hall's whole argument was that the Supreme Court had quietly killed Locke v. Davey across three later cases. The Fourth Circuit said it hadn't — but the same three cases had narrowed it sharply. The band below is that narrowing.

The Fourth Circuit's point is the right-hand edge: narrowed is not the same as dead. As the majority put it, the Supreme Court “has yet to either overrule or abandon” Locke, and under Rodriguez de Quijas a lower court must follow the case that directly controls.

Every narrowing decision was written by the same Chief Justice, and none of them overruled it. That is precisely the Fourth Circuit's point — and precisely Judge Richardson's complaint.

The rule

What Virginia actually excludes

This is the detail most short write-ups compress, and compressing it is what makes the coverage diverge. The exclusion is by major, not by school.

Religious colleges are in

Students at Liberty, Regent, and Eastern Mennonite are all eligible for VTAG. Nothing about attending a religious university disqualifies you.

Religious-vocation majors are out

An eligible institution is one whose primary purpose is collegiate, graduate or professional education “and not to provide religious training or theological education” (Va. Code § 23.1-628). The grant itself may be used only for programs “other than those providing religious training or theological education” (§ 23.1-631(C)). The regulation names CIP Code 39 programs — majors that “prepare individuals for the professional practice of religious vocations” (8 Va. Admin. Code § 40-71-10).

A double major keeps the money

Pair an eligible major with a CIP 39 major and you stay eligible, as long as you don't take more credits in the ineligible one in any semester. The court noted this makes VTAG broader than the Washington program upheld in Locke.

Grant value: up to $5,000 per school year.

The holding

Why she lost

Hall conceded the facts were, in her own brief's words, “on all fours” with Locke v. Davey. So the case turned on one question: had Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza and Carson abrogated Locke — in her phrase, “into oblivion”?

The panel's answer was two words: They did not.

The majority walked each case and found the same thing every time: the Supreme Court distinguished Locke rather than burying it, and three separate times invoked the “historic and substantial state interest” against using taxpayer funds to support church leaders. Hall's analogy to Lemon failed because with Lemon the Court had said out loud that it was abandoned. It has said nothing of the sort about Locke.

We disagree with Hall and hold that the Supreme Court has yet to either overrule or abandon its decision in Locke through Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza, or Carson.

Judge DeAndrea Gist Benjamin, for the panel

The concurrence

The part that made this newsworthy

Judge Richardson joined the majority in full, then wrote separately to say the precedent he had just applied is indefensible. This is the passage that lets an outlet write a completely different headline off the same decision.

I concur because Locke v. Davey controls here. I write separately because Locke was wrongly decided. It betrays the founding generation's commitment to religious liberty, and the Supreme Court should formally overrule it. But, until the Court does, Locke binds us — even as a moth-eaten shell of its former self.

Judge Julius N. Richardson, concurring

He goes further than disagreement. On the counterfactual:

Absent Locke, the Court's more recent decisions in Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza, and Carson would require us to hold that Virginia's program violates the Free Exercise Clause. But we are not yet free to ignore Locke.

And on the institutional question underneath it — when may a lower court treat a precedent as dead? — he reaches for an image that is hard to forget:

The “murderer” kills the case. And the Supreme Court is the only acceptable murderer in our system of strict vertical stare decisis. Yet the murderer is different from the coroner. The coroner declares the case dead. The question … is not whether lower courts can be murderers — they obviously can't. Instead, the question is whether the Supreme Court is the sole coroner, or whether lower courts may sometimes assume that role.

Richardson, J., concurring, n.5

He closes: “Locke v. Davey is a stain on our Free Exercise jurisprudence … But until the Court formally buries Locke, we lower-court judges must keep applying it.” A footnote on Chief Justice Rehnquist's majority reads, in full: “Even a great Chief Justice can err.”

Before you read any of it

There are two cases, and the coverage constantly conflates them

Two separate lawsuits attack the same Virginia exclusion, against the same defendant, both brought by Liberty University students, both lost below, both in the Fourth Circuit. Search results mix them freely.

The one that made law is not the one with the press operation

Hall v. Fleming — Bethany Hall, represented by The National Legal Foundation. This is the case that produced the published opinion and the Richardson concurrence.

Johnson v. Fleming — three different students (Cameron Johnson, Luke Thomas, Trace Stevens), represented by Alliance Defending Freedom, also attacking the National Guard tuition program. Still on appeal as No. 26-1437.

ADF's press shop dominates this subject area, so most of the advocacy-adjacent material — ADF's own release and explainer, The Christian Post, The College Fix, Stars and Stripes, Virginia Lawyers Weekly — is about Johnson. Hall's counsel has a far smaller media footprint, which is why the case that actually made the precedent drew the thinner coverage.

The main finding

The Locke-shaped hole

The sharpest divergence in the coverage isn't left versus right. It tracks who sourced the story.

Coverage written from ADF's press materials does not mention Locke v. Davey at all.

Not in ADF's case explainer. Not in The Christian Post's write-up. Locke is the entire reason both cases lost.

Those pieces frame the dispute as equal treatment — students denied grants for choosing the “wrong” religious degrees, treated as “second-class citizens.” It is a coherent argument. But removing the controlling precedent converts a story about a court bound by 2004 case law into a story about officials singling out religion, and those imply very different things about whether anything can be done.

Every independently reported piece — WORLD, Courthouse News, Religion Clause, Volokh's Short Circuit, Liberty Magazine — puts Locke at the center.

Same day, same holding

Two real headlines

Not a hypothetical this time. Both were published on 13 May 2026 about this decision.

Courthouse News Service · Joe Dodson

Fourth Circuit upholds Virginia policy barring tax dollars for pastoral degrees

The state wins. The subject is public money, and the verb belongs to the court. Hall appears as the object of the sentence, if at all.

Bloomberg Law · Mary Anne Pazanowski

Liberty University Ministry Student Denied Chance at State Grant

A student loses. The subject is her, the verb is passive, and the state never appears. Same court, same day, same holding.

Law.com's National Law Journal went a third way and made the concurrence the whole story: “4th Circuit Judge Urges Supreme Court to Overturn Theology Scholarship Ban Precedent.” (Headline only — the article is behind a block and was not read.)

A quieter difference

How much of a person she's allowed to be

Sympathy in this story is carried almost entirely by biography, and half the coverage declines to supply any. WORLD and Courthouse News make Hall a person. Religion Clause, Liberty Magazine and Volokh identify her only structurally — Religion Clause never names her at all.

WORLD, which interviewed her, reports she is a pastor's daughter who feels called to serve as a worship leader, took on multiple jobs, and is out roughly $20,000.

That's $20,000 that I just lost because I'm studying what I feel like I should do with my life.

Bethany Hall, to WORLD, 19 May 2026

Read the digests and it's a clean doctrinal question about vertical stare decisis. Read WORLD and it's a 20-year-old who lost two years of tuition. Neither is inaccurate.

Corrections worth noting

Errors that survived into print

Found while checking coverage against the opinion.

  1. Courthouse News refers to Hall as “He” in one paragraph of the decision story. She is a woman throughout the opinion.
  2. The same piece says Judge Agee “concurred with the other judges.” Agee wrote nothing — he joined Benjamin's majority. Richardson wrote the concurrence. In the one case where the difference between joining and concurring is the entire story, that swap inverts who said what.
  3. Bloomberg Law's summary has the court calling Locke “perfectly analogous.” The opinion says “directly analogous.” Flagged as unconfirmed — the full article is paywalled and couldn't be read.

One apparent contradiction turns out not to be one. The opinion says Hall “recently completed her sophomore year”; WORLD, six days later, says she “just completed her junior year.” Both are right — the opinion describes the record as pleaded in early 2025, WORLD describes her a year on.

The silence

What nobody wrote

No coverage could be found from the church-state-separation side. Neither Americans United nor the Freedom From Religion Foundation appears to have published anything — despite this being a rare win for the exclusion they defend.

Nothing found either at Reuters Legal, Law360, the ABA Journal, Religion News Service, SCOTUSblog, Inside Higher Ed or Higher Ed Dive. On the other side, nothing from Becket, First Liberty, National Review, The Federalist, the Washington Examiner, The Daily Signal, Baptist Press, Christianity Today, or Liberty University's own newsroom.

So the framing space around this case is occupied almost entirely by one side. Apart from a single Courthouse News headline, a reader will find essentially nobody arguing that Virginia was right.

What happens next

The Supreme Court question is intent, not fact

Hall's lead counsel said on the record that they want the Supreme Court to take it:

The historical analysis in Locke was flawed… We certainly hope that the Supreme Court will grant cert in this case.

Steven W. Fitschen, National Legal Foundation, to Courthouse News, 13 May 2026

But as of 9 August 2026, a contiguous scan of the Supreme Court's own docket API across the OT2025 and OT2026 number ranges — including extension-of-time applications — turns up no petition naming Hall or Fleming. The Fourth Circuit's public RECAP docket likewise shows nothing after the 13 May judgment: no rehearing petition, no en banc petition.

The clock is nearly out

Judgment entered 13 May 2026. Under Supreme Court Rule 13.1 the 90 days run to roughly 11 August 2026 — two days after this page was compiled. A petition could still be timely filed. None is docketed yet.

Caveat worth stating plainly: RECAP mirrors PACER and isn't guaranteed complete, and the Court's docket API is keyed by number rather than party name. Read this as “nothing appears on the public dockets,” not as proof nothing was filed.

Meanwhile the live vehicle may not be Hall's case at all. In the parallel Johnson litigation the district court dismissed the VTAG claims under Locke on 31 March 2026 — but declined to dismiss the claims against Virginia's National Guard tuition program, saying it stayed open to the possibility that some fact might distinguish it from Locke. One plaintiff is a Guard member pursuing an M.Div. to become a military chaplain.

That is a sharper test than Hall's. Locke's whole rationale is a historic interest against spending taxpayer money to train clergy — which is harder to maintain where the government employs the clergy in question. That appeal, No. 26-1437, was fully briefed as of 24 July 2026, with amici including retired U.S. Army Chiefs of Chaplains and a nineteen-state group of attorneys general.

Whether the Court would want it is contested even among sympathisers. Regent's Christian Edmonds told WORLD: “This just doesn't strike me as one that they're going to have an appetite to overturn. They would have done it already if so.”

Other media

You can hear the argument

The Fourth Circuit posts its oral-argument recordings, and this one is public. It is the only non-text media about this case that could be found — and it is more candid than any of the write-ups, because you hear the judges working out that they think Hall might be right and that they can't do anything about it.

Method

How this page was built, and its limits

Everything in the timeline, the holding and the quotations comes from the published opinion, read in full. Where a claim comes from news coverage instead, the outlet is named on the spot.

This page is about a decided federal appeal and how it was reported. It is deliberately not a profile of Bethany Hall. She is a named litigant in a public case, not a public figure, and nothing here goes beyond what the court record and the coverage of the case itself contain.

Nothing here is legal advice, and this is a summary — the opinion is short and worth reading directly.
