The case & the coverage Interview prep

Prepared 9 August 2026

Interview prep.

Everything below is drawn from the published opinion, the public dockets, and the coverage as it actually ran. It is not media training or legal advice. It is a map of what the record supports, what is already wrong in circulation, and the questions that are genuinely hard to answer.

The single most useful thing here: the strongest material in this case is not the outcome. It is a judge who voted against Hall and then spent five pages saying the law that made him do it should be overruled.

Check this first

The Supreme Court line is the one that can go wrong

As of today, nothing is filed

Judgment entered 13 May 2026. Under Supreme Court Rule 13.1 the 90 days run to roughly 11 August 2026 — two days from now.

Checked today across the Supreme Court's docket API: no certiorari petition naming Hall or Fleming, and no extension-of-time application in the 25A series (362 live application dockets scanned). The Fourth Circuit's public docket shows no rehearing or en banc petition either.

Why this matters more than it looks: any reporter can check the Supreme Court docket in about thirty seconds. “We are taking this to the Supreme Court” is a claim that gets falsified live if nothing is on file.

What counsel has actually said on the record is narrower and safe to repeat — Steven Fitschen to Courthouse News on 13 May: “We certainly hope that the Supreme Court will grant cert in this case.” Hoping to petition and having petitioned are different claims.

Re-verify this before any appearance. It is the one fact on this page with a two-day shelf life, and it may well change.

The core

Safe ground and thin ice

Everything on the left is supported by the opinion or a verified docket. Everything on the right is a claim the record does not currently carry.

Supported by the record

  • She lost. The panel affirmed dismissal unanimously.
  • A judge on that panel wrote that Locke v. Davey was “wrongly decided,” is “a stain on our Free Exercise jurisprudence,” and should be formally overruled.
  • That same judge wrote that absent Locke, recent Supreme Court cases “would require us to hold that Virginia's program violates the Free Exercise Clause.”
  • The Supreme Court has narrowed Locke three times and confined it to “vocational religious degrees.”
  • Virginia excludes by major, not by school. Liberty students are eligible; CIP Code 39 majors are not.
  • The grant was worth $5,000 a year, and she went without it for two academic years.
  • At argument, Judge Agee said from the bench she may “well have a winner at the Supreme Court.”

Not currently supported

  • “We have appealed to the Supreme Court.” Nothing is docketed as of today.
  • “The court said Virginia discriminated against me.” The majority held the opposite. Only the concurrence leans her way, and it still voted to affirm.
  • “Virginia won't fund religious students.” It funds them at religious universities. It declines to fund one category of major.
  • “This was a split decision.” It was 3–0. Richardson concurred, he did not dissent.
  • “Locke has been overruled.” Narrowed, repeatedly. Never overruled. That distinction is the entire case.
  • Anything about the three ADF plaintiffs. Different case, different lawyers.

Expect these

The hard questions

Ordered roughly by how uncomfortable they are. The answers here are not scripts, they are what the record will actually bear.

  1. Hardest

    The court pointed out you could have kept the money by double-majoring. Why didn't you?

    This is the sharpest question available and it comes straight out of the opinion. Virginia lets a student combine an eligible major with a CIP 39 major and stay funded, so long as the ineligible major isn't credit-dominant in a given semester. The court used this to say Virginia's program is broader than the Washington one upheld in Locke.

    There is no way to make this go away by argument. The honest answer is about what the workaround costs: it funds her only if she keeps ministry study below half her load, which is a state condition on how much of her degree may be devotional. The objection is to being paid to study less of it, not to the arithmetic.

    Source: slip op. at 3 and n.6, citing 8 Va. Admin. Code § 40-71-10.

  2. Hardest

    Your own briefs conceded your case was identical to Locke. Didn't you concede your way to a loss?

    She did concede it. The district court noted she agreed her situation aligned with Locke's plaintiff, and her reply brief said the facts were “on all fours.” An interviewer who has read the opinion will find this.

    It was a deliberate choice, not a slip. The argument was never that she was distinguishable from Locke — it was that Locke is no longer good law after Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza and Carson. That strategy only works if you accept the case is squarely governed by the precedent you want overruled. It also means a lower court was never able to give her a win.

    Source: slip op. at 4, 7, quoting Hall's reply brief.

  3. Trap

    Should taxpayers pay to train clergy?

    This is the framing that Locke itself rests on, and the one Courthouse News put in its headline. Answering the question as asked concedes the premise, because it accepts that the money is for training clergy rather than for a student attending college.

    The available counter is Judge Richardson's, and it is a judge's words rather than a litigant's: the founding-era history involved laws that singled out ministers for public money, not laws that included them in benefits open to everyone. He quotes Scalia: “No one would seriously contend that the Framers would have barred ministers from using public roads on their way to church.”

  4. Trap

    Isn't this the Alliance Defending Freedom lawsuit?

    No, and this will come up repeatedly, because most of the material online is about the other case. ADF represents Cameron Johnson, Luke Thomas and Trace Stevens in Johnson v. Fleming, which is still on appeal. Hall's counsel is the National Legal Foundation.

    Worth knowing rather than resenting: an interviewer may attribute the other plaintiffs' facts, or ADF's quotes, to her.

  5. Trap

    If a judge agreed with you, why did you lose?

    Because he agreed with her about the law being wrong, not about who should win. Vertical stare decisis means a court of appeals must follow a Supreme Court case that directly controls, even one it thinks is indefensible. Richardson's own line: “until the Court formally buries Locke, we lower-court judges must keep applying it.”

    This is the most sympathetic true thing in the case and it is easy to overstate into something false. He voted against her.

  6. Friendly

    What did this actually cost you?

    Two academic years of a $5,000 grant. She has already told WORLD the figure she uses is roughly $20,000 and that she took on multiple jobs. That is her account, on the record, and repeating it is safe.

    Note the arithmetic gap: $5,000 across two years is $10,000. If she uses the $20,000 figure she should be ready to say what it covers, because it invites the question.

  7. Friendly

    What happens next?

    The honest answer today is that the deadline is days away and nothing is on file. The more interesting answer points at the other case: in Johnson, the district court dismissed the tuition-grant claims under Locke but refused to dismiss the challenge to Virginia's National Guard tuition program, where one plaintiff is a Guard member pursuing an M.Div. to become a military chaplain.

    That is a harder case for the state, because Locke's rationale is a historic objection to public money training clergy — which is awkward where the government employs the chaplains it is declining to help train.

Already wrong in print

Errors she may be asked to confirm

These ran in real coverage. An interviewer working from clips may repeat them, and agreeing on air makes them hers.

  1. Courthouse News refers to her as “He” in one paragraph of its decision story.
  2. The same piece reports that Judge Agee “concurred.” He joined the majority and wrote nothing. Richardson wrote the concurrence. If an interviewer credits the concurrence to the wrong judge, the correction matters, because the whole story is which judge said what.
  3. Bloomberg Law's summary has the court calling Locke “perfectly analogous.” The opinion says “directly analogous.” Minor, and flagged as unconfirmed since the full piece is paywalled.
  4. Her year of study will look inconsistent across clips. The opinion says she recently completed her sophomore year; WORLD, six days later, says junior. Both are right, because the opinion describes the record as pleaded in early 2025.

Know the room

Two kinds of interviewer

Coverage split cleanly, and the split predicts the questions.

Read the ruling

Will ask about Locke

Courthouse News, Bloomberg Law, Religion Clause, Volokh, Liberty Magazine. They know she conceded the case was governed by Locke, and they will ask about vertical stare decisis and the double-major provision. Technical, and mostly fair.

Read a press release

Will not mention Locke at all

Coverage sourced from ADF's materials omits the precedent that decided the case, and frames it as officials treating religious students as second-class citizens. Expect “why is Virginia hostile to Christians” — a question whose honest answer is that the judges were bound, and one of them objected at length.

The second kind is friendlier and more dangerous. A sympathetic premise she doesn't correct becomes a quote she owns.
